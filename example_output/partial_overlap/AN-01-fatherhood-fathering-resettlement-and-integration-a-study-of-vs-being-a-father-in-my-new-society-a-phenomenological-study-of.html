2 Abstract People across the world flee their homes and countries because of wars, conflicts, natural disasters, or social unrest to seek asylum in other countries. The migration journey of refugees is a long and diverse one and their settlement in a new society creates new life opportunities, as well as generates many challenges. Fathers experience this journey in their own ways. This study is at the intersection of important considerations for social sciences: forced migration, fathering, social inclusion, and wellbeing. Framed by phenomenology and symbolic interactionism, this study documents the migration journey of eighteen Syrian refugee fathers and investigates how the meaning associated with fatherhood and its cultural transmission impacts their fathering practices in the Canadian context. It documents the shifting meaning of fatherhood alongside the migration trajectories of these fathers. The study also identifies the challenges they are facing throughout the transition and resettlement process. Methods This research involved in-depth interviews conducted between November 2020 and March 2021 with 18 Syrian refugee fathers who were resettled in Canada. The interviews explored their migration journey, the meaning they gave to their fathering practices during this migration journey, the settlement reality, and the challenges they faced and are still facing as fathers in their new society. The transcriptions of these interviews were submitted to an interpretative phenomenological analysis. The material was coded, and emergent and superordinate themes identified for each dimension of the study using NVIVO. Findings The fathers’ migration journey was categorized into pre-flight, life in transition and settlement stages. The pre-arrival stage involves fleeing their country of origin of Syria, life in transition includes their lives in a transitional country, and the challenges they faced, where fathers are in limbo, uncertain and not able to practice their fatherhood as they expected to according to their culture. The resettlement stage is a new beginning and “a new birth” where these fathers and their families arrive with no knowledge of Canadian society, have to learn a new language, and adapt to a new culture. Syrian refugee fathers face many challenges throughout their migration journey and settlement in Canada. Syrian refugee fathers associate fatherhood with being a provider and the authority figure of the family as transmitted through their customs, traditions and religious beliefs. They believe that being a father is to be available, to make decisions and to be a mentor to their children as they transmit their cultural heritage and teach their children to behave in society and become respectable citizens. The main challenges of being a Syrian father in Canadian society are revisiting the meaning of fatherhood and of gender roles and developing new parenting skills. Challenges such as employment and learning a new language are among the many challenges they face during the post arrival stage.
